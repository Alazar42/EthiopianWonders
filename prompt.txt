# Project: Ethiopian Wonders — Spherical Endless Runner

## 1. Your role and mission

Act as a senior Godot 4.x game developer, gameplay programmer, technical game designer, procedural-generation engineer, and low-poly environment artist.

Build a complete, playable 3D game for a game jam with the theme **Tourism + AI**. The game will showcase the wonders of Ethiopia through an endless, three-lane running experience set across small spherical planets inspired by the gravity and planetary traversal mechanics of Super Mario Galaxy, combined with the automatic forward movement and three-lane controls of Subway Surfers.

This is an implementation task, not a planning-only task. Inspect the existing repository, replace its old project structure and implementation, create the necessary scenes and scripts, run the game, test the mechanics, and fix errors.

Do not stop after producing a plan, architecture document, mockup, or a collection of disconnected scripts. Implement the actual playable game.

## 2. Core game concept

The player automatically runs along the surface of a large spherical planet. The camera follows from a carefully chosen fixed gameplay angle. The world has three lanes that curve with the planet's surface.

The player can move left and right between the three lanes. They do not manually control forward movement or the camera.

As the player travels, procedural generation creates new sections of the route ahead. These sections can contain straight paths, curved paths, sharp turns, Ethiopian tourist landmarks, and transitions between spherical planets.

There are no coins, no coin collection system, and no conventional obstacle course. The primary gameplay objective is to discover Ethiopian wonders.

When an Ethiopian wonder approaches, a recognizable target appears in the world. The player must click the target before passing the landmark's interaction deadline. Successful discoveries unlock an achievement and a short educational fact about the wonder.

The player starts with three hearts. Missing or failing to discover three targets ends the run.

The experience should feel like an endless adventure through a chain of miniature worlds, with Ethiopian tourism as the central identity.

## 7. Spherical planet mechanics

This is the most important technical system.

Do not create a flat runner track and merely place a sphere underneath it. The player must actually travel over a curved planetary surface.

### Planet representation

Each planet must have:

- A center position.
- A radius.
- A visual sphere or suitable spherical terrain mesh.
- A traversable surface.
- A local gravity direction.
- A defined route and lane coordinate system.
- Optional landmark placements and planet-specific visual settings.

For the initial implementation, use spherical planets with a radius substantially greater than the capsule height and lane spacing.

For example, start with a planet radius of approximately 35–60 world units and tune the player dimensions and camera to achieve the desired visual scale.

The planet must visibly read as a large world relative to the player, not as a tiny ball.

### Local gravity

For a spherical planet with center `C` and player position `P`, calculate the local outward direction as:

`up = normalize(P - C)`

The inward gravitational direction is:

`gravity_direction = -up`

Apply gravity consistently with the planet's local coordinate frame.

The player must remain aligned to the local surface normal. As the player travels around the sphere, the player's local up direction must rotate accordingly.

Avoid applying world-down gravity while expecting the player to remain attached to the sphere.

Use a stable character controller or an equivalent custom movement controller suitable for planetary gravity. If using `CharacterBody3D`, implement and test custom up-direction handling, gravity, and movement relative to the current surface normal.

Do not assume ordinary flat-world `move_and_slide()` behavior alone solves planetary movement.

### Planet-relative movement

Define the forward direction relative to the current route, not as a fixed global axis.

Project the route's forward direction onto the tangent plane of the sphere. Normalize it, then derive the lateral direction using the local surface frame.

The movement frame must remain stable as the player moves around the sphere.

Do not repeatedly calculate left and right using an arbitrary global up vector, because that will cause the lane directions to become unstable when the planet's surface normal changes.

Use a robust orthonormal local frame or a parallel-transport approach to preserve the direction of travel across curved surfaces and sharp turns.

The result must support a player running along a curved route while staying grounded on the planet.

### Planet traversal

Allow the player to travel over substantial portions of a sphere, including around its sides where appropriate.

The gameplay camera and the local coordinate frame must remain understandable as the player moves.

Prevent sudden 180-degree rotations, jittering, unwanted lane inversion, and unexpected camera flips.

If a route leaves the traversable surface, the player must follow the designed transition or fall in a controlled manner. Do not silently teleport the player to the opposite side of the planet.

## 8. Three-lane runner movement

The player moves forward automatically at a controlled speed.

The only required movement input is left and right.

Use keyboard controls:

- Left Arrow or A: move one lane left.
- Right Arrow or D: move one lane right.

Also support equivalent controller left/right input if practical.

The lane system contains exactly three logical lanes:

- Left.
- Center.
- Right.

Clamp lane selection so the player cannot move beyond the outer lanes.

Lane switching should feel responsive and smooth, with controlled interpolation rather than instantaneous lateral teleportation.

The lateral motion must follow the local planetary surface rather than a fixed world-space X coordinate.

When the route bends, the lane positions must bend with it.

Do not add manual forward, backward, free-look, or mouse camera movement controls.

### Jumping between planets

The player may jump from one planet to another, but the primary movement input must remain left and right.

Implement planet-to-planet traversal as a designed gameplay event:

- A transition segment appears ahead.
- The destination planet is visible before departure.
- The route and destination are aligned so the player can understand the connection.
- A controlled launch or automatic jump sequence moves the player between planets.
- The player's gravity and local orientation transition from the source planet to the destination planet.
- The player lands on the intended lane of the destination planet.
- The forward-running behavior resumes smoothly.

Do not require a jump button for the initial version. Use authored launch points or automatic launch events.

A later extension may add manual jumping, but it must not complicate the core controls.

If a direct jump is too far, use a visible stylized launch bridge or a short, designed airborne transition rather than relying on an unreliable ballistic jump.

## 9. Camera system

The camera must evoke the readability and spectacle of a third-person planetary platformer.

Use a fixed, carefully chosen gameplay composition that follows the player.

Requirements:

- Place the camera behind and above the player at a tested angle.
- Keep the player and the forward path visible.
- Show enough of the planet's curvature to communicate the spherical world.
- Keep approaching landmark targets visible long enough for the player to click them.
- Smoothly follow the player's movement.
- Blend the camera when changing planets or when the route changes orientation.
- Avoid sudden snapping, excessive camera shake, and distracting zooms.
- Do not allow the mouse to rotate, orbit, or otherwise control the gameplay camera.
- Do not implement free-look camera controls.
- Ensure the camera remains usable on straight segments, sharp turns, and planetary transitions.

A fixed gameplay camera does not mean the camera must retain a fixed world-space rotation regardless of the planet. It means the player cannot control the camera and its framing is deliberately managed by the game.

When the player travels onto a differently oriented planet, smoothly update the camera's reference frame to preserve a consistent view of the player and the route.

## 10. Procedural endless route generation

Build an endless route system that generates content ahead of the player and removes content behind them.

Do not instantiate a complete world or infinite collection of planets at startup.

Use a deterministic, segment-based procedural generator.

### Segment types

Implement reusable segment types:

1. Straight three-lane segment.
2. Gentle curved segment.
3. Left-turn segment.
4. Right-turn segment.
5. Sharp left turn.
6. Sharp right turn.
7. Landmark approach segment.
8. Planet transition segment.
9. New-planet arrival segment.
10. Short recovery segment after a demanding turn or transition.

These segments must connect using explicit start and end frames. Each segment should define its incoming orientation, outgoing orientation, length or arc distance, lane layout, and compatible successor types.

Use a weighted transition system with constraints instead of choosing arbitrary segments independently.

For example:

- A sharp turn must have a valid incoming and outgoing direction.
- A planet transition must have a valid destination.
- A landmark approach must leave enough time for the player to identify and click its target.
- A difficult sequence should not repeatedly generate impossible turns.
- A transition should not cause the player to spawn outside the playable surface.

### Sharp turns

Sharp turns must change the actual route direction, not just rotate decorative geometry.

The three lanes must follow the turn.

Use authored curved path geometry or sampled route frames. The player should continue moving along the new forward direction without a sudden sideways teleport.

Support deliberate 90-degree-style turns in the local route frame, while keeping the player on the sphere's surface.

Use a small amount of easing when appropriate, but retain a recognizable sharp-turn feel.

Ensure that lane input remains relative to the route after a turn.

### Continuous generation

Maintain a configurable number of route segments ahead of the player.

As the player progresses:

1. Check the generation distance.
2. Generate additional compatible segments when required.
3. Register new landmarks and planet transitions.
4. Update the active route and lane information.
5. Remove segments that are sufficiently far behind.
6. Free their nodes and release their references.

Avoid accumulating thousands of inactive nodes during a long run.

Use object pooling only if profiling shows that it is beneficial. Start with reliable instantiation and cleanup.

The player must never reach the end of the currently generated route during normal gameplay.

### Planet selection

Generate a continuous journey across a changing collection of planets.

Planets may vary in radius, color, terrain material, background composition, and available landmark placements within reasonable bounds.

Use a configurable planet catalog. The first planet should be a safe tutorial planet with a straight segment and an introductory discovery target.

Introduce sharper turns and planet transitions gradually.

Keep the first playable version deterministic enough to debug. Add seeded randomness so a run can be reproduced when testing a specific generation sequence.

## 11. Landmark targets and discovery interaction

This is the central tourism mechanic.

There are no coins and no conventional collectible currency.

A landmark target is a clickable in-world object associated with a specific Ethiopian wonder.

Examples of future landmark content may include Lalibela, the Simien Mountains, the Rock-Hewn Churches, the Aksum obelisks, and other Ethiopian cultural or natural wonders. Use generic placeholders until the actual assets and verified content are provided.

### Target behavior

When a landmark approaches:

- Show a recognizable target in the world.
- Place it in a visible, reachable location associated with the route.
- Make it clear that it is clickable.
- Give it a subtle visual emphasis, such as a ring, floating marker, or pulsing low-poly icon.
- Keep it large enough to click reliably.
- Ensure it is not obscured by the capsule or terrain.
- Make the target available only during its valid interaction window.

The player can click the target using the mouse.

Mouse input is reserved for selecting the landmark target and interacting with menus. It must not control the camera or player movement.

### Interaction deadline

A target must have a defined interaction window.

The window begins when the landmark becomes available and ends when the player passes the designated landmark boundary.

Use route progress or a well-defined trigger to determine when the player has passed the target.

Do not use arbitrary screen coordinates or a fixed timer alone to determine whether a target was passed.

On click:

1. Verify that the target is still active.
2. Verify that the player has not passed its interaction boundary.
3. Verify that the click selected the actual target.
4. Mark the target as discovered.
5. Prevent duplicate clicks from awarding the same discovery multiple times.
6. Trigger the achievement and knowledge popup.
7. Update the player's discovery record.

A click on empty space must not count as a discovery.

Clicking after a target has passed must not retroactively award it.

### Target implementation

Use a dedicated `Area3D`, collision shape, or another reliable Godot picking mechanism.

Handle mouse picking through a dedicated raycast from the gameplay camera or a tested Godot input-picking approach.

Make sure the world-space ray reaches the intended target and does not select the planet, the player, or an unrelated object instead.

Avoid relying on a global mouse click handler that marks whichever target happens to exist.

### Discovery success

On a successful click:

- Play a brief visual confirmation.
- Mark the target as completed.
- Add the wonder to the discovered-wonders collection.
- Award an achievement.
- Show a knowledge popup.
- Continue the run without unnecessarily blocking movement.

The popup must not require the player to stop running. It should remain readable briefly and dismiss itself or allow a simple close action.

## 12. Three-heart failure system

The player begins with exactly three hearts.

A target that passes without being discovered counts as one failed discovery and removes one heart.

A target that is clicked incorrectly or after it has passed must not grant an achievement. Do not count random clicks on empty space as missed discoveries.

When a landmark's interaction window expires without a successful discovery:

1. Mark that landmark as missed.
2. Remove one heart.
3. Show a brief, clear feedback animation or message.
4. Continue the run if hearts remain.

When all three hearts are lost:

- Stop forward movement.
- Stop procedural generation.
- Disable active targets.
- Show the game-over screen.
- Display the number of wonders discovered and the final run statistics.
- Provide Restart and Main Menu buttons.

The three hearts represent failed landmark discoveries, not collisions with obstacles.

Do not introduce obstacles or collisions that damage the player in the initial implementation.
